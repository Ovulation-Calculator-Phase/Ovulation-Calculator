What is an ovulation calculator?

An ovulation calculator is a calendar tool that estimates when ovulation may occur from the first day of a period, a typical cycle length, and an assumed luteal phase. It does not observe hormones or confirm that an egg was released. Its result is a planning range that can help you understand how cycle dates relate to one another.

This calculator shows an estimated ovulation date, a seven-day fertile window, the next expected period, and a planning due date. It also lays out those markers for seven projected cycles. The dates are calculated in your browser, and the result should be read alongside the limits described below.

How to use the ovulation calculator

Use the form with the cycle information that best represents your recent pattern. The calculator accepts a cycle length from 21 through 45 days and a luteal phase from 10 through 16 days.

  1. Enter the first day of your last period, meaning day one of menstrual bleeding.
  2. Choose your typical cycle length. Count from the first day of one period to the day before the next period.
  3. Choose an estimated luteal phase. Fourteen days is a common planning value, but the tool lets you select 10 to 16 days.
  4. Check My cycles are irregular if your cycle lengths change. The result remains a calendar estimate and includes a reliability reminder.
  5. Select Remember my details on this device only if you want the browser to retain the values. You can remove them with Forget my data.

Press Show my fertile window to generate the calendar. You can copy the summary, download calendar events, print the results, or start over without sending period dates to a server.

How to read your results

Estimated ovulation

The estimated ovulation date is the day produced by counting back from the expected next period by your selected luteal phase. It is a central point for the calendar, not a test result. A later or earlier actual ovulation can move every related date.

Fertile window

The calculator marks five days before estimated ovulation through one day after it. This seven-day display is intentionally a little wider than the egg's survival time because cycle timing and observations are uncertain. It is not a guarantee of fertility on every marked day.

Expected period

The next-period estimate adds the selected cycle length to the period start date. Each later card repeats that interval, so changes in actual cycle length can make future cards diverge from your experience.

Planning due date

The planning due date adds 280 days to the period start date. It is a conventional calendar estimate, not confirmation of pregnancy and not a substitute for clinical dating.

Seven-cycle calendar

The calendar cards show the same markers for seven cycles so you can see how a consistent assumed length projects forward. Treat the later cycles as increasingly dependent on the assumptions you entered.

How the dates are estimated

The calculator uses simple date arithmetic. It first projects the next period, then counts backward for the luteal phase, and finally expands the fertile window around the estimated ovulation date.

What is ovulation?

Ovulation is the release of an egg from an ovary. A rise in luteinizing hormone, often called the LH surge, commonly occurs before ovulation and is one reason urine LH tests can provide information about approaching fertile days. The exact sequence and timing can differ between people and between cycles.

After release, an egg is usually viable for roughly 12–24 hours. Sperm can survive longer in the reproductive tract, which is why the days before ovulation matter when describing the fertile window. A calendar cannot tell whether ovulation happened, and symptoms can have more than one cause.

The fertile window explained

The fertile window describes the days when intercourse may overlap with sperm survival and the short period when an egg remains viable. Research by Wilcox and colleagues found that the highest probability of conception in a cycle was concentrated in the few days before ovulation and on the estimated ovulation day. This evidence describes population patterns, not an individual prediction.

Our range runs from five days before estimated ovulation through one day after. The extra day gives the calendar room for uncertainty in the estimated date and the short post-ovulation viability of an egg. A marked day is not a promise that ovulation occurred or that pregnancy will or will not result.

For a separate explanation of calendar markers, see the ovulation calendar guide. The calculator is not contraception.

Signs of ovulation

Some people notice changes near ovulation, although no single sign confirms that ovulation occurred.

  • Cervical mucus: discharge may become wetter, clearer, and more slippery.
  • Basal body temperature: a sustained rise of about 0.3 °C / 0.5 °F may appear after ovulation.
  • LH test: a urine test can detect an LH surge that often precedes ovulation.
  • One-sided pain: brief pelvic discomfort can occur, but it is not specific to ovulation.

Illness, sleep changes, medicines, and other conditions can affect observations. See the guide to ovulation symptoms for context.

When calendar estimates are less reliable

Calendar estimates are less dependable when cycle lengths vary substantially. Postpartum changes, breastfeeding, polycystic ovary syndrome, perimenopause, illness, and hormonal contraception can all affect bleeding patterns or ovulation timing. A long cycle may also trigger a reliability warning in this calculator.

You can still record dates and use the irregular-cycle option, but do not treat a projected date as confirmation. The guide to irregular periods explains why additional context may matter.

When to take a pregnancy test

Home pregnancy tests are generally most useful from the first day of a missed period. Follow the instructions for the specific kit, including any guidance about repeat testing if the first result is negative. A calculator's fertile-window date cannot diagnose pregnancy. See when to take a pregnancy test for more information.

When to talk to a healthcare professional

Consider discussing fertility evaluation after 12 months of trying when under 35, or after 6 months when age 35 or older. Seek individual care sooner for very irregular cycles, severe or persistent pain, unusual bleeding, or other concerns. The fertility evaluation guide describes general thresholds and does not replace a clinical assessment.

Ovulation calculator FAQ

Are these dates exact?

No. They are calendar estimates, and timing can vary from cycle to cycle. A calendar cannot confirm that ovulation occurred.

Can I use this with irregular cycles?

You can, but the estimate may be less reliable when cycle lengths vary. The irregular-cycle option adds a reminder that the result is a planning estimate.

Does this calculator prevent pregnancy?

No. Calendar estimates are not contraception and should not be used to prevent pregnancy.

Are my dates sent to a server?

No. Inputs run in your browser. If you choose Remember my details on this device, the values are saved in this browser's local storage until you remove them.

How long does ovulation last?

The egg is usually viable for roughly 12 to 24 hours after release. The fertile window is wider because sperm may remain capable of fertilization for several days before ovulation.

Can I get pregnant right after my period?

It is possible, especially with shorter cycles or earlier-than-expected ovulation. Sperm can survive for several days, so bleeding dates alone cannot rule out a fertile window.

How accurate is an ovulation calculator?

It provides a calendar estimate based on the information you enter, not a measurement of hormones or ovulation. Accuracy is lower when cycles are irregular or the selected luteal phase does not reflect your pattern.
